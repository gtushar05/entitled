# Entitled — defense pack

Everything you need to present and defend this project in an interview. Every
number here is produced by a script in `scripts/` and pinned by a test.

## CV bullets

**One-liner (tightest):**

> **Entitled** — version-aware LLM agent for Indian Railways refunds where a
> *deterministic* calculator owns every rupee (LLM only parses & explains,
> behind a numeric-faithfulness gate); 70-case frozen golden oracle at 100%
> exact / 0 unsafe outputs, and an ablation showing an LLM *alone* gets 31% of
> trap cases right vs. the system's 100%.

**Two-line variant (more surface area):**

> **Entitled — rules-grounded refund agent (Indian Railways).** Deterministic,
> clause-cited calculator across 3 statutory regimes owns all arithmetic; the
> LLM only does NL extraction (97% outcome accuracy) and rephrasing, gated by a
> numeric-faithfulness check (κ=0.64 vs. an independent LLM judge). 70-case

## 90-second demo script

1. **The hook (15s).** "It answers 'how much refund do I get for cancelling my
   train ticket' — but the rules changed twice in 2026, and IRCTC's own website
   still shows the old ones. So the interesting part isn't the LLM; it's that a
   *deterministic calculator* does the money, version-aware by date and train,
   and the LLM is never trusted with a number."

2. **The version-aware trap, live (25s).** Calculator tab: same ₹2,400 2A
   ticket, cancelled early. Regular train → charge **₹200** (flat, 2015 rule).
   Switch train type to Vande Bharat Sleeper → charge **₹600** (25%, Jan-2026
   rule 6(4)(a)). "Same ticket, different regime, cited to the gazette clause.
   A reader of IRCTC's site gets the second one wrong."

3. **It refuses to guess (15s).** Show an Apr-2026 flat-decided case → it
   **escalates**, because that regime's flat minimums aren't primary-verified
   yet. "It knows what it doesn't know — a pre-committed rule, not a vibe."

4. **The money slide (25s).** "I measured whether you even need the calculator.
   Give an LLM the exact clauses but no calculator: **31%** correct on the trap
   cases vs. the system's **100%**. It didn't hallucinate wrong amounts — it got
   *paralyzed*, denying refunds that were actually due. The 200-line calculator
   makes that 100%, for free, and every answer is auditable to a clause."

5. **Close (10s).** "70-case frozen golden oracle, zero unsafe outputs, 133
   tests, CI gate that re-checks the oracle on every push. Same doctrine as my
   other project, Incremental, in a totally different domain."

## The gauntlet — hard questions & honest answers

**Q: It's 2026, models are great. Why not just prompt Claude with the rules?**
I measured that. Given the exact governing clauses in context but no calculator,
an LLM got **31%** of the 29 trap cases fully right vs. the verified system's
100%. The honest detail: it didn't fabricate wrong numbers (0 unsafe outputs) —
it was *paralyzed*, abstaining on 12 refunds that were actually due. So a
pure-LLM product wouldn't overpay the railway; it would frustrate passengers by
denying legitimate refunds, and still be wrong two-thirds of the time on the
hard cases. Beyond accuracy: the calculator is **deterministic** (same input →
same rupee, auditable), **version-aware** (it tracks which statutory regime
applies — a model can't cite a rule version it wasn't handed), and **free**.

**Q: 100% on your own golden set — isn't that circular / overfit?**
The discipline is what prevents that. Every expected value in the golden set is
**hand-computed from the rules as a literal**, never generated by the calculator
— a golden set derived from the code under test can't catch that code's bugs.
`reconcile_golden.py` diffs the hand values against the live agent (a mismatch
means either my arithmetic slip or a real bug), it's **SHA-256-stamped** so it
can't drift silently, and nothing is ever *tuned* against it — it's an oracle,
not training data. The traps were deliberately chosen where official sources are
currently wrong. And CI re-runs the reconcile on every push.

**Q: Your own ablation shows hybrid retrieval ≈ BM25. So the dense layer is dead
weight — why keep it?** I'd rather report that straight than dress it up. On this
31-clause corpus BM25 already handles the clause-terminology queries the agent
generates; dense embeddings only help on low-overlap paraphrases, and with the
MiniLM backend that win doesn't even survive RRF fusion. I keep dense as an
optional, gracefully-degrading layer (it matters more as the corpus grows and on
paraphrase-heavy input), but the key point is **blast radius**: retrieval only
*explains* — citations bind by exact clause id emitted by the calculator, so a
retrieval miss can never corrupt a rupee figure. Measuring it and finding it a
wash is the finding.

**Q: You only tested Haiku for the LLM-only baseline. A frontier model would
nail the traps.** Probably closes some of the gap — I flagged that caveat in the
repo. But it doesn't change the design decision: you shouldn't have to *gamble*
on a frontier model's arithmetic (and pay frontier prices) for something a
200-line function does exactly and for free. And correctness is only one axis —
determinism, auditability, and clause-level citation for a regulated calculation
are independent of model quality. My provider frontier makes the same point from
the other side: correctness is **model-independent** here, so Haiku is the
rational default at ~2.6× lower cost.

**Q: Your faithfulness validator misses value-swaps. Isn't that a hole in the
core safety claim?** Yes, and it's documented and pinned by a test so it can't
regress silently. A token-set check literally can't see a refund↔charge swap
because both numbers are already in the allowed set. That's *why* there's a
second line of defense: an independent LLM judge caught all 4 swaps the validator
missed, and the κ=0.64 quantifies how much the free deterministic gate already
buys before you pay for the judge. Note the swap is a narrower failure than
fabrication — it needs the model to keep both correct numbers *and* transpose
their roles — and fabrication (the common case) is caught.

**Q: The April-2026 regime just escalates — so your system can't actually answer
it.** By design, and that's the feature. The percentage tiers are confirmed by
the government's announcement, but the primary circular isn't published, so any
branch whose answer depends on the unconfirmed flat minimums escalates — a
**pre-committed** rule, decided before I saw any case, not a dodge at answer
time. The alternative is guessing the flat minimums, which is exactly the
unsafe behavior the whole project exists to avoid. When the circular lands, those
branches flip to COMPUTED with a one-line change.

**Q: Extraction is 97%, not 100%. What happens on the 3%?** They fail *safe*.
The two misses were disruption cases whose phrasing stated no cancellation time,
so the agent returned NEEDS_INFO (asked) rather than inventing one. Structurally,
a bad extraction can only ever produce a wrong *input*, which the deterministic
"Understood: …" echo surfaces to the user, and which the calculator's validation
often rejects — never a silently wrong rupee figure, because the LLM's words
never reach the arithmetic.

**Q: 31 clauses, one domain — is this a toy?** The clause count is small; the
*architecture* is the transferable part: a verified component owns the decision,
the LLM is boxed in by validation on the way in and a faithfulness gate on the
way out, a hand-adjudicated frozen oracle guards regressions, and every source is
cached with provenance. Scaling is more clauses and regimes, not a redesign. The
proof it generalizes is my sibling project **Incremental** — same doctrine, a
completely different domain (causal uplift), same guarded-LLM + verified-core +
pre-registered-oracle shape.

**Q: Why would a fintech/product company care about railway refunds?** It's a
template for any **regulated calculation over messy natural-language input** —
KYC eligibility, credit-policy decisions, insurance claims, tax. Anywhere the LLM
must handle free text and cite authority but must *not* be trusted with the
number. That's exactly the shape of the credit-policy and bureau-rule work I did
at Slice; this is that pattern, built clean and measured end to end.

## The Incremental mock, revisited

The Incremental mock opened with: *"You built a causal targeting engine. My
response models already make money. Why should I care?"* The answer, crisply:
a response model ranks who is **likely to convert**; it happily spends budget on
**sure things who'd convert anyway** and on **lost causes**. Uplift ranks who
converts *because of* the treatment — the persuadables. On the sealed Criteo
holdout that reweighting was worth a **+1.17pp** uplift@10% over the propensity
baseline (paired CI [+0.89, +1.48], separable), and at an 8% budget the
uplift-chosen policy returned **₹480K vs. ₹327K** for the response-model policy
and **−₹577K** for treat-everyone. "Your response model makes money; it also
wastes a measurable slice of budget on people whose minds were already made up —
here's the number, on pre-registered data."

Same underlying pitch in both projects: **don't optimize the proxy (likelihood,
or fluent prose) when you can optimize and verify the real thing (incremental
value, or the exact rupee).**
